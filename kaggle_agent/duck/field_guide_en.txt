
Field guide (patterns seen across many ARC-AGI-3 games; treat as hypotheses to confirm, never as facts):
- These are small human-designed puzzle games: always solvable, level 1 is a tutorial, and each later level usually adds exactly one new mechanic on top of the previous ones. Reuse what you learned, but re-check it on the new board.
- Probe before you theorize. In the first turns, try one of each: every distinct kind of object, an object's centre, an object's edge, and plain empty ground far from everything. Clicking empty ground can move the currently selected piece there, and a click that "does nothing" often means the move was blocked (wall, board edge, hazard) or needs a precondition (something selected first, a matching colour, a water level): repeat the same action 2-3 times, from another spot or in another direction, before concluding it has no effect.
- Only after the probes ask "what is this level asking me to do". A common goal shape: make the region you CAN change look like the region you CANNOT change (answer area vs. pattern/template). Cells no action ever changes are the template.
- Any family label below is a hypothesis. One contradicting observation is enough to drop it; do not keep repeating the same click on the same target under a hypothesis that has already failed once.
- Families seen so far, described by what they LOOK like (not how to play them):
  A) Copy the pattern: a template and an answer area side by side; a "brush" whose kind (big / small / colour change / pick-a-part) must be discovered; later paint covers earlier paint.
  B) Navigate and unlock: arrow keys move one ~5x5 block; a small corner panel shows the block's current shape/colour, a room's display shows a target shape; map gadgets change shape/colour; an energy bar drains while walking and empties you back to the start.
  C) Push the box: a movable block and a slot; pushing only, no pulling.
  D) Lights-out: clicking one cell flips a region; the flip pattern is fixed and learnable.
  E) Dictionary translation: paired examples plus a fill-in area and a cycle key; the level completes by itself when everything matches.
  F) Draw a spell: a small canvas, a hint pattern, a character and an exit; the drawing casts a skill, the exit is reached by the character.
  G) Chain reaction / collect: one click triggers a large cascade, or scattered pieces must be gathered into slots.
  H) Extend-and-retract pipes: coloured 3-wide segments joined end to end; per colour a box with a shorten cell and a lengthen cell (3 cells per press); a small box rotates a segment and everything after it; the goal is the tip (small dot near the end) on a diamond marker; walls block growth; same-colour segments share one box; all targets must hold at the same time.
  I) Tanks and boats: black = water, thin light lines = pipes, square buttons in pairs flanking a pipe pour water between the two tanks at its ends (total water conserved); a boat floats; a plug inside a pipe is a gate that moves the boat when the water on both sides is level with it (the move plays as an animation over the next frames); win = each boat in the row of its same-coloured gate.
  J) Move-the-pieces-to-balance: several same-coloured diamonds ("stations") with one selected; clicking anywhere moves the selected station there; a small solid diamond sits at the centroid of the group and must land inside a hollow diamond of the same colour; large tinted regions are hazards for that centroid piece; a column of white cells at the left edge counts remaining clicks.
- Counters: a row or column near an edge that changes every step regardless of what you do is a step or life counter; do not treat it as a mechanic, but do treat it as a budget.
- If something does not change on the very next frame, do not conclude it did nothing: some effects (gates, animations, level transitions) show up a step or two later.
